# Strategic C-terminal Degeneration of TP53 Confers Cancer Resistance in Pigs: A “Less-Is-More” Evolutionary Mechanism

## Abstract
The occurrence of cancer does not scale with body size or lifespan in animals, a phenomenon known as Peto’s paradox. Large mammals like elephants have evolved extra copies of the TP53 tumor suppressor gene to mitigate cancer risk. Intriguingly, domestic pigs (Sus scrofa) appear to have low natural cancer incidence despite their large body mass and long lifespan, and yet they lack TP53 duplications. We propose that pigs may instead employ a “less-is-more” strategy by simplifying the TP53 protein’s regulatory C-terminal domain (CTD). To test this hypothesis, we outline a comparative analysis of TP53 sequences across vertebrates. We will compare domain-specific GC content, predict post-translational modifications (PTMs), model structural features, and assess selection pressure on the TP53 CTD. This framework will reveal whether pig TP53 has undergone adaptive CTD degeneration—reducing regulatory complexity while preserving core DNA-binding function—to confer cancer resistance.

## Introduction
Peto’s Paradox. According to Peto’s paradox, larger or longer-lived species do not inevitably have higher cancer rates, contrary to expectations based on cell number and division count. For example, African elephants evolved ~20 copies of TP53 coincident with the emergence of large body size, resulting in enhanced DNA-damage responses. This expansion of TP53 appears to resolve Peto’s paradox in elephants by increasing tumor suppression capacity. Cancer Resistance in Pigs. Domestic pigs (Sus scrofa) are comparable to humans in size (often >100 kg) and thus have a very large number of cells. Surprisingly, spontaneous tumors in pigs are extremely rare: one review of slaughterhouse records found cancer occurrences in young pigs to be <0.005%. Likewise, detailed surveys report only ≈40 cases per million animals. These observations suggest that pigs have effective anticancer mechanisms, even though their genome shows no TP53 copy-number expansion like in elephants. The genetic basis for this putative cancer resistance in pigs is unknown. It cannot involve extra TP53 genes, so pigs may have evolved other molecular adaptations. The p53 C-terminal Domain. The TP53 protein has multiple functional domains: an N-terminal transactivation region, a central DNA-binding domain (DBD), an oligomerization domain (OD), and a C-terminal domain (CTD) (the final ~30 aa). The CTD of p53 is a short (~30 aa) intrinsically disordered segment that is rich in regulatory phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination sites. This disordered tail participates in nearly all aspects of p53’s transcriptional activity and regulation. In humans, the CTD contains many modifiable residues that can either promote or inhibit DNA binding and stability. If pigs have altered or degenerated CTDs, this could significantly change how p53 is regulated without altering its core DNA-binding function. Hypothesis and Objectives. We hypothesize that pigs have adopted a “less-is-more” evolutionary strategy: rather than increasing TP53 copy number, they streamline the existing TP53 gene by losing regulatory complexity in the CTD. In other words, targeted degeneration (e.g. loss of PTM sites or structural order) in the pig p53 CTD may enhance tumor suppression by reducing unwanted regulatory “noise” while preserving the conserved DNA-binding core. This contrasts with other lineages: elephants expanded TP53 copies (retaining complex regulation), and some species (e.g. fish) may even lose p53 functionality entirely. To investigate this, we will perform a cross-species analysis with the following aims:

- **Comparative genomics:** Collect full-length TP53 coding sequences from diverse vertebrates (mammals such as human, cow, mouse, rat, dog, cat, horse, macaque, gorilla; pig; and distant species like zebrafish) to provide evolutionary context.
- **Domain-specific composition:** Compute nucleotide frequencies and GC-content for the entire CDS and separately for the CTD of each species’ TP53 gene. We will quantify ∆GC% (GC_CTD – GC_CDS) to detect shifts in base composition that may affect codon usage or methylation.
- **PTM site prediction:** Identify candidate phosphorylation (S/T/Y), acetylation (K), and ubiquitination (K) motifs in each TP53 CTD using motif-scanning tools. We will compare the total number and conservation of these PTM sites across species.
- **Structural properties:** Model the pig TP53 CTD structure (e.g. by homology modeling or predictive folding) and compute features such as intrinsic disorder, helix content, and backbone flexibility. Comparison with human p53 CTD will reveal changes in secondary structure or dynamics.
- **Selection analysis:** Use codon-based evolutionary models (PAML/codeml) to estimate nonsynonymous/synonymous rate ratios (dN/dS) for the CTD region in pig versus other species. Elevated dN/dS in pig CTD would indicate positive selection driving change.
- **Phylogenetic context:** Build a maximum-likelihood phylogeny of TP53 coding sequences (with bootstrapping) to contextualize our findings. We will classify evolutionary strategies among lineages (e.g. “conservation” vs. “duplication” vs. “degeneration”).

All analyses will be performed in codon space (preserving reading frame). The domain boundaries will be defined by alignment to the human reference (p53 CTD ≈ residues 364–393). Methods such as MAFFT (for alignment) and IQ-TREE (for phylogeny) will be used, along with Biopython scripts for composition and custom code for PTM scanning and modeling. PAML (codeml) will estimate selective pressures. In summary, we will integrate sequence, structural, and evolutionary analyses to test whether the pig TP53 CTD shows evidence of adaptive simplification.

## Methods
- **Sequence collection:** We retrieved full-length TP53 coding sequences (CDS) from NCBI RefSeq (NM accessions) for each target species (e.g. Homo sapiens, Sus scrofa, Bos taurus, Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus, Canis lupus, Felis catus, Equus caballus, Macaca mulatta, Gorilla gorilla, and Danio rerio). Only high-quality transcripts were used.
- **Multiple alignment and phylogeny:** Sequences were aligned at the codon level using MAFFT. A best-fit codon substitution model was selected, and a maximum-likelihood TP53 phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE with 1,000 bootstrap replicates. The tree will be visualized to highlight clade-specific patterns.
- **Domain segmentation:** Based on the human p53 reference (UniProt P04637), we defined domain boundaries as follows: the N-terminal transactivation domain (NTD), core DNA-binding domain (DBD), oligomerization domain (OD), and C-terminal domain (CTD) (the final ~30 aa). We mapped these boundaries onto each aligned CDS to extract the CTD region for further analysis.
- **GC content and base composition:** Using Biopython, we computed nucleotide frequencies and GC% for the full TP53 CDS and for each CTD segment. The difference in GC content (∆GC% = GC_CTD – GC_CDS) was calculated for all species. We will compare ∆GC% values and overall base composition across lineages.
- **PTM motif scanning:** We scanned each TP53 sequence for canonical motifs of regulatory PTMs: serine/threonine/tyrosine phosphorylation motifs (e.g. [STY]-[P/Q/M] for proline-directed kinases), lysine acetylation, and lysine ubiquitination sites. Predicted sites were tabulated by domain and species. Loss or conservation of key PTM residues will be noted.
- **Structural modeling:** We generated a structural model of the pig TP53 CTD using homology modeling or ab initio prediction, aligned to the known human p53 CTD conformation. We will compute root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and compare secondary structure content (helical vs. coil) between pig and human CTDs. Intrinsic disorder scores (via tools like IUPred) and solvent accessibility will be assessed.
- **Selection tests (dN/dS):** Using the codon alignment of TP53 CTDs, we will apply PAML’s codeml to estimate branch- and site-specific dN/dS ratios. We will specifically test whether the pig CTD shows dN/dS > 1 (indicating positive selection) compared to the largely conserved DBD.
- **Visualization and summarization:** Analysis outputs (alignments, trees, tables of GC% and PTMs, structural metrics) will be compiled. We will create summary tables (e.g. CSV) and publication-quality figures to illustrate cross-species comparisons of TP53 composition, PTM sites, and structural features.

For example, we will compute ∆GC% for the pig CTD relative to its coding region and compare it to human and other mammals. These data will be summarized in a table, highlighting any unusual GC shifts in pig CTD.
- **Evolutionary pressure (dN/dS):** We will present codeml results for dN/dS in the CTD. If the pig CTD has been adaptively evolving, we expect a higher dN/dS value for pig compared to other species. We will report branch-specific dN/dS estimates and test for statistically significant positive selection on the pig lineage.
- **PTM site differences:** We will list the number of predicted phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination motifs in the CTD of each species (e.g. human vs pig). We will highlight which regulatory residues are conserved or lost in pigs. For instance, we may find that the pig CTD lacks many serine or lysine residues present in human p53, indicating a loss of potential PTM sites.
- **Structural features:** We will compare structural models of pig and human CTDs. Metrics such as RMSD between pig and human structures, percentage of alpha-helix, and disorder score will be reported. For example, if the pig CTD is more disordered (lower helix content, higher flexibility) than human, this will be quantified.
- **Phylogenetic context:** The TP53 phylogenetic tree will be annotated to show branches with TP53 duplication (e.g. elephants), conservation, or degeneration. We anticipate categorizing three strategies: “Conservation” (e.g. primates, rodents, with highly conserved CTDs), “Duplication” (elephants, with many TP53 copies), and “Degeneration” (pigs, possibly some fish) based on combined evidence.

These results will test our hypothesis that pig TP53 exhibits targeted CTD simplification. The key findings (∆GC%, PTM loss, structural differences, and dN/dS) will determine if the “less-is-more” strategy is supported.

## Discussion
Our planned analyses will reveal whether pig TP53 has uniquely streamlined its C-terminal domain, consistent with an adaptive “less-is-more” strategy. If pigs show a significantly lower number of regulatory PTM sites in the CTD than humans (and other mammals), this would indicate loss of potential modification hotspots. In that scenario, pig p53 might be less prone to dysregulation by signaling pathways, focusing its activity on core DNA-binding functions. Elevated dN/dS in the pig CTD would suggest that changes were driven by selection rather than neutral drift. The CTD of p53 is known to be a major site of regulation: it is intrinsically disordered and interacts with numerous cofactors. Streamlining this region could be advantageous by reducing complexity. This idea echoes the broader concept that loss-of-function changes can be adaptive. In mammals, targeted gene losses have been linked to adaptations (the “less-is-more” principle). Here, instead of losing an entire gene, pigs may have lost regulatory segments within a gene. Comparatively, elephants retain a complex TP53 network (many copies and intact regulatory tails) to achieve tumor suppression, whereas some vertebrates may tolerate TP53 reduction or loss (e.g. certain fish species). If pigs truly simplify TP53 regulation, this would represent a distinct evolutionary path: eliminating excess regulatory sites in a critical tumor suppressor, rather than adding gene copies. Such a mechanism could reduce opportunities for oncogenic mis-regulation. These insights could have broader implications. Understanding the minimal regulatory requirements of p53 for effective tumor suppression might inform synthetic biology or cancer therapies. For example, designing a “stripped-down” p53 with fewer PTM sites could yield a robust tumor suppressor protein with lower risk of dysregulation. More generally, this work highlights how comparative genomics can uncover unconventional cancer defense strategies. The pig’s unique biology may thus provide valuable lessons for evolutionary medicine and oncology.

## Conclusion
In summary, we have outlined a comprehensive cross-species analysis to test whether Sus scrofa avoids cancer via strategic simplification of the TP53 C-terminal domain. By examining sequence composition, predicted PTMs, structural properties, and selection signals, we aim to determine if pigs have sacrificed CTD complexity to preserve core tumor-suppressing function. This “less-is-more” approach, if validated, would be a novel evolutionary solution to Peto’s paradox. It expands our understanding of how large animals can resist cancer without gene duplication, offering a new paradigm for studying and potentially engineering p53 in medical research.
